# Interview Philosophy

Before Potential is a product, it's a point of view about what an interview is actually for. This document is about that — not the software, the conversation it's built around.

## The question worth asking

Most interviews, without meaning to, end up answering the wrong question. They optimize for _"did this person perform well just now, under pressure, in a room with a stranger?"_ — which is a real thing, but a narrow and often misleading one. Confidence, fluency, and familiarity with interview conventions all shape that answer, and none of them are the thing being hired for.

Potential is built around a different question:

> Have we collected enough trustworthy evidence to fairly understand this candidate's capability?

That's a question about the interviewer's own preparation and attention as much as it is about the candidate. It can be answered honestly with "not yet," which "did they do well?" rarely allows for. We think that honesty is the whole value of asking it this way.

## Genuine capability looks like specifics

Confident-sounding answers and well-evidenced ones are easy to mistake for each other in the room, and much easier to tell apart afterward, on paper. Genuine capability tends to show up in specifics — a real decision, a real trade-off, a moment where something went wrong and the candidate describes exactly what they did about it. Vague competence-shaped language ("I'm a strong communicator," "I always collaborate well") is not evidence of anything, no matter how fluently it's delivered.

This is why Potential is built to notice the specific and set aside the generic. A quote and a reason are worth more than an overall impression, because a quote can be checked by anyone reading it later, and an impression can't.

## Objectives keep the conversation honest

An interview without clear objectives tends to drift toward whatever's easy to talk about, which usually isn't the same as what the role actually needs. Potential is built around a fixed, known set of competencies and objectives for each interview, decided ahead of time — not discovered mid-conversation, not expanded on the fly to fit whatever the candidate happens to be good at talking about.

That structure isn't there to make the interview feel like a checklist. It's there so that "we didn't get to it" is a visible, nameable gap, instead of something that quietly falls through the cracks of an unstructured conversation.

## Evidence gaps are normal, not a failure

Most single responses won't fully cover every competency an interview is meant to explore, and that's expected, not a problem to paper over. Potential's job at that point is to say so plainly — which competency still lacks support, and specifically why — so the interviewer can decide what to do about it, rather than discovering the gap only when it's too late to ask.

Naming a gap clearly is a form of respect for the candidate, too: it means nobody gets judged on a competency they were never actually given a fair chance to demonstrate.

## A good next question is earned, not generic

"Tell me about a time you faced a challenge" can be asked in any interview, about anything, and rarely produces evidence specific enough to be worth much. A good follow-up question does the opposite — it picks up something the candidate already said and asks them to go one layer deeper into it, aimed at whatever's still missing.

That's the only kind of follow-up Potential is built to suggest: one question, grounded in what was actually said, aimed at a real and current gap. Not a bank of behavioral prompts. Not several options to pick from. The interviewer still decides whether to ask it, rephrase it, or ask something else entirely — the suggestion is a starting point for their judgment, not a script.

## The interviewer is the instrument

None of this replaces the interviewer's own read of the conversation — the hesitation before an answer, the tone, the things that don't fit neatly into a competency. Potential is built to make sure the _evidence_ side of that judgment is as complete and precise as it can be, so the interviewer's own instincts have better material to work with, not less to do.

A good interview, in the end, is one where the candidate got a genuine chance to show what they can actually do, and the interviewer walked away with something concrete enough to explain their decision to someone who wasn't in the room. Potential exists to help with exactly that — by collecting better evidence, not by deciding what it means.
