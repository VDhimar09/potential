# Manifesto

Every interview ends the same way: someone has to decide.

Before that moment, though, comes a much longer one — the part where a candidate is actually talking, explaining a decision they made, describing a project that went sideways, walking through how they thought. That part is where the truth lives. Almost everything built to help with hiring skips past it, racing toward a score, a rank, a verdict, as if the deciding were the hard part and the listening was just preamble.

We think it's the other way around.

Deciding is easy once you've actually understood someone. What's hard — what's usually done badly — is listening carefully enough, and for long enough, to know whether you've understood them at all. Most interview tooling doesn't help with that. It helps you decide faster, which is not the same thing as helping you decide well.

Potential exists because we think that's backwards.

We started from a single question, and we've tried to never drift from it:

> **Have we collected enough trustworthy evidence to fairly understand this candidate's capability?**

Not: did they perform well. Not: what's their score. Not: would we hire them. Just — do we actually have enough to go on, and is what we have any good.

That question has consequences. It means the thing worth building isn't a smarter verdict — it's a better memory. Something that notices what a candidate actually said, holds onto it precisely, notices what's still missing, and hands all of it back so a human can read it and think it through themselves. Evidence, not opinion. A quote and a reason, not a number.

It also means being disciplined about what we won't build, even when it would be easy to. A model that can extract evidence from a response can, with very little extra effort, be made to score that response too. We think that's a trap. A score feels like more help than a quote and a reason, but it's actually less — it's a conclusion with the reasoning sanded off, and it quietly asks the interviewer to trust it instead of checking it. Evidence asks to be checked. That's the whole point.

So Potential's AI has one job: pay closer attention than a person can while they're also busy conducting the interview, and hand back what it noticed — grounded, specific, inspectable. It doesn't grade the candidate. It doesn't rank them against anyone else. It doesn't tell you who to hire. When it isn't sure something counts as evidence, it says so, rather than guessing to seem useful.

That leaves the hard part exactly where it belongs: with the people in the room. They're the ones who heard the tone, felt the hesitation, know what the role actually needs. No system watching a transcript should take that away from them, and we've built Potential so that it structurally can't.

We think this is what responsible AI in hiring actually looks like — not a system that promises to be fair by making the call for you, but one that makes it easier for a human to be fair, by giving them better material to decide from.

Potential exists to help interviewers uncover genuine capability by collecting better evidence — not by making better hiring decisions.

Everything else follows from that.
